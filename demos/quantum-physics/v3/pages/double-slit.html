Lab 03 // the famous one

One particle, going through
both slits at once.

Send single electrons at a wall with two gaps. Each one lands as a single dot, like a particle. But fire thousands and the dots stack into stripes, the signature of a wave. Each particle somehow went through both slits and interfered with itself. Then watch what happens when you peek.

Each dot is one whole particle. The stripes only exist across many. Flip the detector on and the stripes collapse into two plain blobs, one behind each slit.

The build-up is the whole point

Watch the screen with the fire rate low. The first dozen hits look random, scattered like buckshot. There is no way to predict where the next one lands. Yet the crowd has a plan: bright bands where the two paths add up, dark gaps where they cancel. Probability with structure.

PROBABILITY CURVE

Why peeking kills it

To know which slit a particle used, you have to interact with it, bounce a photon off it, tag it somehow. That nudge scrambles the delicate phase relationship between the two paths. With the which-path information available, the wave story is gone and you get particle stripes: two heaps, no interference. Information has a physical price.

"It has in it the heart of quantum mechanics. It is the only mystery." — Feynman

Richard Feynman called this experiment the central mystery of the whole field, and he meant it. Nothing about it can be explained the classical way. The particle is not secretly going through one slit and we just don't know which. The math only works if it genuinely explores both and the two possibilities interfere. The instant you force an answer to "which one?", reality obliges, and the magic stops.
