Lab 01 // two faces

It travels like a wave.
It arrives like a dot.

Louis de Broglie's wild 1924 guess: everything that moves is also a wave, even you, just with a wavelength too tiny to notice. For an electron the wave is huge by comparison. The trick to making a "particle" out of waves is to stack many wavelengths so they pile up in one spot. That moving lump is a wave packet, and it is the closest thing quantum mechanics has to a particle.

Stack just one wave and it fills all of space, total wave, no location. Stack many and they cancel everywhere except one bump, which then looks and moves like a particle. More waves, sharper bump.

de Broglie's bargain

The faster something moves, the shorter its wavelength: wavelength equals Planck's constant divided by momentum. A thrown baseball has a wavelength of about 10⁻³⁴ metres, unmeasurably small, so it looks purely like a particle. An electron's wavelength is roomy enough to diffract through a crystal, which is exactly how electron microscopes work.

λ vs MOMENTUM

Sharper in space, blurrier in speed

To localise the packet tightly you must blend a wide range of wavelengths, which means a wide range of momenta. Squeeze the packet narrow and its velocity becomes uncertain. You just discovered the uncertainty principle by accident, it falls straight out of stacking waves.

The question "is it a wave or a particle?" has the wrong shape.

It is neither, in the way those words mean for everyday objects. It is a quantum thing that spreads and interferes like a wave while it travels and deposits its energy in one lump when it lands. The two faces are not a contradiction, they are two questions you can ask. Ask about its path and you get wave behaviour. Ask where it hit and you get a single point.
