Lab 08 // the beam splits

Measure its spin and the
beam splits clean in two.

Electrons and atoms carry a tiny built-in magnetism called spin. Send a beam through a lopsided magnet and classically you'd expect a smeared streak as spins point every which way. Instead the beam splits into exactly two sharp spots: spin-up and spin-down, nothing between. Spin is quantized. Chain the magnets at different angles and something even stranger happens to the atom's memory.

Two Z-magnets in a row: the second never flips a spin back. But put an X-magnet between two Z-magnets and the atom forgets its Z answer entirely. Measuring one axis erases the other.

Two values, never a smear

In 1922 Stern and Gerlach fired silver atoms through their magnet expecting a vertical smudge. They got two distinct dots. An electron's spin along any axis you choose is always either fully up or fully down, never tilted, never partial. It is the cleanest demonstration that some quantities in nature simply do not come in continuous amounts.

CLASSICAL vs QUANTUM SPOTS

Measuring erases memory

Select only spin-up along Z, then measure along X, then measure Z again. Half come out down, even though you'd already filtered them all to up. The X-measurement wiped the Z information clean. Spin along different axes are incompatible questions: answering one randomizes the other, the same trade you saw in the uncertainty lab.

Spin is not a tiny ball actually spinning. It is its own kind of thing.

The name is a historical accident. A literal spinning electron would have to whirl faster than light to make the magnetism we measure. Spin is a genuinely quantum property with no everyday picture, yet it is utterly real: it underlies magnets, the structure of the periodic table, MRI scanners, and the very rule that keeps two electrons from sitting in the same state.
