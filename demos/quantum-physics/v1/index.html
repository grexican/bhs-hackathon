What Is Quantum Physics?

Quantum physics (also called quantum mechanics) is the branch of science that describes how matter and energy behave at the tiniest scales — the level of atoms and subatomic particles. At this scale, the familiar rules of everyday life break down and are replaced by surprising, often counterintuitive behavior.

Developed in the early 20th century by scientists like Max Planck, Albert Einstein, Niels Bohr, and Erwin Schrödinger, quantum theory is one of the most successful and precisely tested theories in all of science.

Key Concepts

Quantization

Energy doesn't flow smoothly; it comes in tiny, discrete packets called quanta. This is where the field gets its name.

Wave-Particle Duality

Particles like electrons and photons can behave like both particles and waves, depending on how you observe them.

Superposition

A quantum system can exist in multiple states at once until it is measured — the famous idea behind "Schrödinger's cat."

Uncertainty Principle

Formulated by Werner Heisenberg, it states that you cannot know both the exact position and the exact momentum of a particle at the same time.

Entanglement

Two particles can become linked so that measuring one instantly affects the other, no matter how far apart they are — what Einstein called "spooky action at a distance."

Famous Experiments

  • The Double-Slit Experiment — demonstrated that particles produce wave-like interference patterns, the cornerstone of wave-particle duality.
  • The Photoelectric Effect — explained by Einstein in 1905, showing that light delivers energy in discrete quanta (photons).
  • Stern–Gerlach Experiment — revealed that particles have a quantized property called "spin."
  • Bell Test Experiments — confirmed that quantum entanglement is real and cannot be explained by hidden classical variables.

Everyday Applications

Quantum physics isn't just abstract theory — it powers much of modern technology:

  • Computers & smartphones rely on transistors, which work because of quantum behavior in semiconductors.
  • Lasers in everything from barcode scanners to surgery use quantum principles.
  • MRI machines in hospitals use quantum spin to image the body.
  • Quantum computers use superposition and entanglement to solve certain problems far faster than ordinary computers.
